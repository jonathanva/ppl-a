# Operational Procedures — course-topic summary

[← Back to index](README.md)

**Course scope:** PIC responsibilities and documents; noise abatement; wind shear; wake turbulence; precautionary/emergency landings; ditching; fire/CO; contaminated runways; and survival/reporting topics.

## 1. Preparation and the pilot in command

| Before flight | Check |
|---|---|
| Pilot / aircraft | Fitness, documents, airworthiness/defects, POH/AFM and checklist |
| Route / environment | Weather, NOTAMs, airspace, route and fuel |
| Loading / performance | Mass and balance; take-off and landing performance |
| People / cabin | Brief belts, exits, emergency actions and equipment; secure baggage |

The PIC remains responsible for safe conduct, regardless of ATC clearance or passenger request.

Noise-abatement routes and profiles reduce disturbance but never override obstacle clearance, aircraft limitations or safety. Avoid unnecessary low flight over built-up areas; use published departure/arrival procedures and the approved aircraft profile.

## 2. Wind shear, microburst, and wake turbulence

**Wind shear** is a rapid change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance:

| Initial wind change on approach | Initial aircraft response |
|---|---|
| Headwind increases | IAS/lift may increase; path tends high |
| Headwind decreases or tailwind increases | IAS/lift may decrease; path tends low |

**Microburst sequence:** increasing headwind → downdraft → decreasing headwind / tailwind. This is especially dangerous near the ground. Avoid storms and reported severe shear; if encountered, use the aircraft's approved escape procedure. Do not improvise flap/gear changes or a turn at low altitude.

**Wake turbulence** is the pair of vortices produced by a lifting wing. It is strongest behind a heavy, clean, slow aircraft. Vortices descend and drift with wind; a light crosswind may leave one over the runway. For departure behind a larger aircraft, aim to lift off before its rotation point and climb above its path where safe; for landing, remain above its approach path and touch down beyond its touchdown point. Follow ATC spacing and local procedures. Jet blast is a separate exhaust hazard on the ground.

| Following a larger aircraft | Flight-path picture | Ground reference |
|---|---|---|
| Departing behind a departure | Climb above its path where safe | Rotate before its rotation point |
| Landing behind an arrival | Stay above its approach path | Touch down beyond its touchdown point |

Published separation, ATC instructions, runway length and the aircraft's performance still govern.

## 3. Emergency and precautionary landings

| Landing | When | Field assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency | Circumstances force a landing | Size/shape, slope, surface/crops, obstacles and people |
| Precautionary | Chosen before a situation forces one | Wind, approach path, escape route and available height/time |

Control the aircraft first; make calls and use the checklist when workload permits.

For ditching/over-water exposure, plan survival equipment for all occupants as required by the applicable rules and route. Brief exits and flotation. A life jacket inflated inside the cabin can obstruct egress; inflate it after leaving the aircraft unless the approved procedure directs otherwise. Aircraft-specific ditching speeds, flap, door and evacuation actions are from the POH/AFM.

## 4. Fire, smoke, and carbon monoxide

Fire response depends on the source. A suspected electrical fire may require electrical isolation; an engine/exhaust fire or cabin smoke may require fuel, mixture, heat/ventilation or engine actions in a different order. Use the checklist for the actual aircraft. Do not restart an engine that has been on fire unless the approved procedure specifically directs it. Water is generally unsuitable for electrical/fuel fires; use the installed extinguisher as trained, ventilate only as directed and land as soon as practicable.

Carbon monoxide is colourless/odourless and may enter through a failed heat exchanger. Headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion are warning signs. Stop the suspected source, ventilate/use oxygen if available as directed, land promptly and seek medical help.

## 5. Runway condition and performance

Water, slush, snow, ice, frost or other contaminants can increase rolling resistance, reduce braking and directional control, and increase take-off/landing distance. Standing water can cause aquaplaning: tyre contact and braking effectiveness fall, so stopping distance increases. Wet grass also degrades performance. Read the runway condition report by runway third and use current aircraft performance data; a runway's “wet” label alone is not enough to compute distance.

Operational surface descriptions include **damp** (moisture changes the surface colour), **wet** (soaked but without significant standing-water patches) and water patches. The course/bank definition of a contaminated runway is more than 25% of the runway area being used covered by water, slush, snow or ice deeper than 3 mm; verify the current applicable definition and reporting rule. Learn runway-condition codes from the current source rather than inferring braking from a surface word.
