# Air Law — course-topic summary

**Course scope:** international and European rule structure; rules of the air; collision avoidance; aerodromes; airspace; altimetry; VFR minima; AIS; and local procedures. This follows the repository's Belgian course notes while keeping operational rules anchored to current EASA/state sources.

## 1. Who makes the rules?

The **Chicago Convention** establishes the international framework for civil aviation. **ICAO** publishes Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) in Annexes; states implement them and may notify differences. An ICAO Annex is not itself the same thing as a directly applicable EU regulation. In the EU framework, the Basic Regulation supports implementing rules such as **Part-FCL** (flight crew licensing) and **SERA** (rules of the air). EASA develops technical material and publishes consolidated Easy Access Rules; **AMC** describes an acceptable way to show compliance and **GM** explains the requirements.

For the Belgian course context, distinguish the federal competent authority (**DGLV/BCAA**, under FOD Mobility) from **Skeyes**, the air navigation service provider. Regional authorities have responsibilities connected with regional aerodromes. These names explain the local system; use the current authority and AIP for operational decisions.

## 2. Collision avoidance and aerodrome operations

Right-of-way is not permission to endanger another aircraft. Apply the specific encounter rule:

- **Head-on:** both aircraft alter course to the right.
- **Converging:** the aircraft that has the other on its right gives way, subject to the stated aircraft categories and special cases.
- **Overtaking:** the overtaking aircraft keeps clear and passes to the right.
- Aircraft towing, gliders, balloons and airships have priority relationships set out in SERA; a powered aeroplane must not assume it has priority merely because it can manoeuvre.

Know the circuit sequence—departure/upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final—and determine circuit direction and height from published local information. A **PAPI** gives glidepath information: 2 red/2 white is on slope, more red indicates low, more white high. It does not replace a stabilized approach or the aircraft's visual/airspeed checks.

## 3. Airspace is three-dimensional

A charted boundary has a horizontal footprint **and vertical limits**. Read the class, base, top, lateral extent, activation times, entry requirements and published frequency. CTRs are surface-based control zones; CTAs/TMAs and airways may have stepped bases and tops. A route can be outside a volume laterally but inside it vertically—or the reverse. Temporary restrictions and activity times may be in NOTAMs, not the chart.

For every airspace-crossing question, establish: **where am I; what class/volume; what altitude reference; is it active; what clearance, radio, transponder or flight plan is required?** VFR availability and services differ by class. ATC clearance does not remove the pilot's duty to remain safe and comply with the clearance.

## 4. VFR weather minima and cruising levels

Airspace classes differ in access and ATS:

| Class | VFR access and service (summary) |
|---|---|
| A | IFR only; VFR not permitted. |
| B | VFR permitted subject to clearance; all flights receive separation. |
| C | VFR requires clearance/radio; ATC separates VFR from IFR and provides traffic information between VFR flights. |
| D | VFR requires clearance/radio; separation is generally between IFR flights, with traffic information to VFR. |
| E | IFR is controlled; VFR generally needs no clearance, but is not separated from other VFR. |
| F/G | Advisory/information services; no ATC separation for VFR. |

Services and class availability must be checked against the actual chart/AIP. VMC minima also depend on **altitude band, class, speed category, and state-specific rule**. The course examples use 5 km visibility below FL100 and 8 km at/above FL100; standard cloud separation is taught as 1500 m horizontal/1000 ft vertical, with special low-level and Class B wording in the bank. Always identify class and altitude first, then apply the current SERA/state table; “clear of cloud and surface in sight” is not interchangeable with standard separation.

The semi-circular cruising-level system is based on magnetic track/course. The course diagram uses 000°–179° eastbound and 180°–359° westbound, with odd/even thousand-foot sets respectively; VFR levels may add 500 ft where the applicable rule requires it. Confirm the relevant altitude, route direction and local exception rather than treating a classroom diagram as universal.

## 5. Altimetry and aeronautical information

- **QNH:** altimeter indicates elevation on the ground and altitude above mean sea level in flight.
- **QFE:** indicates height above the selected aerodrome/reference datum.
- **1013.25 hPa / QNE:** standard setting used to reference flight levels.
- A **transition altitude** is the published altitude for changing to standard pressure on climb; the **transition level** is the published level for changing back to QNH on descent. The layer between them prevents conflicting altitude/flight-level references.

Use the current AIP for local TA/TL and QNH. **AIP** contains enduring aeronautical information; **NOTAM** announces time-critical changes; **AIRAC** is the coordinated cycle for significant information changes. Confirm chart edition, NOTAM validity and aerodrome status. A runway condition report gives surface condition by runway thirds; use the current report and aircraft performance data rather than guessing braking performance from a word such as “wet.”
